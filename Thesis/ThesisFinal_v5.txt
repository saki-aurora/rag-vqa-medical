Ethics Statement

Abstract

Medical Visual Question Answering (MedVQA) promises a more useful
interface for clinical AI than standalone classifiers because it allows
clinicians to query images directly in natural language. In
gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy, this capability is especially relevant
for high-volume, high-variability workflows such as ulcerative colitis
severity grading, polyp assessment, and procedure documentation.
However, practical MedVQA remains limited by dataset bias, long-tail
label imbalance, weak lexical grounding in free-text generation, and
inconsistent clinical faithfulness across question types.

This dissertation investigates these limitations and develops a
clinically oriented VQA framework for colonoscopy. The work is grounded
in multi-dataset experimentation across HyperKvasir, Kvasir-VQA,
Kvasir-VQA-x1, ImageCLEF MEDVQA-GI 2023, and LIMUC, covering both
closed-set and generative settings. Repository benchmarks show a clear
pattern: supervised or constrained models remain substantially stronger
than raw zero-shot VLMs on current GI tasks. In our saved runs, ViLT
reaches 0.9089 accuracy on ImageCLEF MEDVQA-GI validation, while
Qwen2.5-VL zero-shot raw outputs collapse to 0.0007 accuracy before
lexical projection. On HyperKvasir, fine-tuned ResNet50 reaches 0.8789
test accuracy, while BLIP2 zero-shot projected labels remain low
(0.0638). On LIMUC severity grading, fine-tuned ResNet50 attains 0.7539
accuracy, macro-F1 0.6829, and QWK 0.8351, with persistent
minority-class difficulty.

These findings motivate the thesis direction: move from score-only
pipelines toward clinically grounded, evidence-aware multimodal
reasoning that combines (1) robust visual recognition, (2)
question-aware answer generation, and (3) retrieval-supported
justification for higher-risk decisions. The dissertation contributes a
structured research agenda, scenario-driven evaluation design, and an
implementation pathway for integrating VQA with retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) in GI decision support. The long-term objective is an
assistant that does not only classify findings, but can explain, qualify
uncertainty, and align outputs with clinician workflow and
evidence-based care.

Keywords: medical visual question answering, gastrointestinal endoscopy,
colonoscopy, ulcerative colitis, vision-language models,
retrieval-augmented generation, clinical decision support

1.0 Chapter 1 Overview

Chapter 1 defines the full research foundation of this dissertation and
clarifies why GI-endoscopy MedVQA must be evaluated beyond aggregate
benchmark scores. The chapter first establishes the clinical motivation
for question-driven AI support in colonoscopy, then formalizes the
central methodological gap: current systems can produce strong average
performance while still failing on clinically high-risk, imbalanced, or
format-sensitive cases. It then translates this gap into a concrete
research program by stating the thesis aim, objectives, research
questions, and working hypotheses. In addition, Chapter 1 sets the
operational boundaries of the work, including what is in scope, what is
explicitly out of scope, and how PICO-oriented evidence mapping is used
as a controlled extension pathway rather than a replacement for visual
reliability. Finally, the chapter introduces the scenario-driven
framing, conceptual pipeline, and risk-control logic that guide all
subsequent experiments and interpretations.

At a glance, this chapter:

3. Formalizes aim, objectives, and research questions with evaluation

1.1 Background & Motivation

Medical Visual Question Answering (MedVQA) is an interdisciplinary field
that integrates computer vision (CV), natural language processing (NLP),
and clinical medicine. It aims to build artificial intelligence systems
that can interpret medical images and answer clinically relevant
questions expressed in natural language. In contrast to conventional
image classification systems that return only categorical predictions,
MedVQA is designed to support question-driven interaction. This
interaction model is closely aligned with how clinicians reason in
practice, where interpretation is typically guided by targeted questions
rather than isolated labels.

MedVQA offers substantial potential for clinical decision support. Its
expected benefits include improved diagnostic consistency, reduced
physician workload, and wider access to specialist-level interpretation
in telemedicine and resource-constrained settings. The long-term
objective is not to replace clinical judgment, but to provide an
intelligent and transparent assistant that can complement clinical
reasoning within existing workflows.

Unlike general-domain VQA, MedVQA faces domain-specific constraints.
Medical images often include subtle, overlapping, or low-contrast
findings that require specialized interpretation. Clinical questions
frequently require multi-step reasoning, in which visual evidence must
be combined with biomedical context and task intent. These
characteristics make MedVQA both a technically demanding multimodal task
and a clinically sensitive reliability challenge.

The technical foundation of MedVQA combines CV-based image
representation with NLP-based question understanding and answer
synthesis. Early systems were predominantly discriminative, using an
image encoder, a question encoder, a fusion module, and a fixed answer
set. These systems performed adequately on constrained tasks but showed
limited flexibility for nuanced and open-ended clinical questions [1],
[2], [3]. Recent work has shifted toward transformer-based multimodal
architectures and generative response models, supported by large-scale
vision-language pretraining [4], [5] and contemporary MedVQA surveys of
large multimodal model behavior [8], [9]. In parallel, biomedical
language models such as BioBERT and BioGPT have improved domain-specific
language understanding and generation [6], [7].

Despite this progress, the gap between benchmark performance and
reliable clinical utility remains significant. Three persistent tensions
define the present landscape:

Expressiveness versus reliability: Generative models can produce richer
answers, but may exhibit lexical drift, weak grounding, or hallucinated
content.

Aggregate performance versus clinical robustness: High overall accuracy
can conceal clinically critical failures in minority classes.

General multimodal capability versus medical domain specificity:
Zero-shot transfer from general VLMs often underperforms in specialized
clinical settings.

This dissertation is motivated by these tensions and investigates them
in the context of gastrointestinal endoscopy, with a specific focus on
colonoscopy-oriented MedVQA.

1.1.1 From Fixed-Answer Systems to Generative Multimodal Models

The historical evolution of MedVQA provides an important context for
this thesis. In early systems, answer generation was operationally
equivalent to label selection. This formulation enabled straightforward
optimization and evaluation, but it constrained clinically meaningful
expression. Complex questions were often reduced to coarse categorical
outputs, limiting interpretability and reducing practical value for
clinicians.

Transformer-era multimodal models improved cross-modal alignment and
enabled free-text response generation. This transition addressed
important limitations of earlier systems, particularly weak
question-image interaction and restricted output vocabulary. However, it
also introduced new risks. In clinical settings, a fluent answer that is
weakly grounded in image evidence may be more problematic than a
constrained but verifiable answer. Consequently, model expressiveness
must be balanced with grounding, calibration, and error control.

Another major shift has occurred at the evaluation level. Instead of
reporting performance within a single benchmark only, recent studies
increasingly examine cross-dataset behavior and robustness to
distributional changes. This is particularly relevant in GI endoscopy,
where data heterogeneity in question templates, answer conventions, and
visual characteristics can be substantial.

1.1.2 Limitations of Aggregate Benchmark Scores in Clinical AI

Benchmark metrics remain necessary, but they are not sufficient for
clinical validation. A model can achieve high overall accuracy while
failing on rare yet clinically consequential categories. Weighted scores
may obscure failures in underrepresented classes, and single-point
metrics may not capture the clinical implications of specific error
types.

For severity-oriented tasks, ordinal relationships are also important.
Confusing adjacent grades is not equivalent to missing severe disease
altogether. Therefore, evaluation design in clinical MedVQA should
include classwise metrics, imbalance-aware metrics, ordinally sensitive
metrics, and scenario-level acceptance logic.

This dissertation adopts that broader evaluation view from Chapter 1
onward. In addition, answer format itself is treated as an evaluable
design choice. For certain use cases, label-only output may be
sufficient; for others, clinicians may require rationale, confidence
estimates, or evidence pointers. A reliable clinical assistant must be
assessed on both predictive accuracy and output usability.

1.2 Why GI Endoscopy and Colonoscopy

Gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy is a strategically important target for
MedVQA from both clinical and methodological perspectives.

Clinically, colonoscopy supports screening, diagnosis, and follow-up
across multiple disease contexts, including colorectal neoplasia and
inflammatory bowel disease. Endoscopic interpretation directly affects
treatment escalation, surveillance decisions, and procedural reporting.
In routine practice, clinicians frequently pose focused questions such
as:

- 
- 
- 
- 
- 

Is an abnormality present?How many findings are visible?Where is the
lesion located?What type or morphology is observed?What is the likely
severity of inflammation?These question types map naturally to MedVQA
task families and are suitable for systematic evaluation.

From a technical perspective, GI endoscopy is challenging. Image quality
can vary due to motion blur, specular reflections, debris, variable
bowel preparation, and viewpoint variation. Question-answer
distributions can be highly imbalanced, with clinically important severe
classes often underrepresented. Consequently, colonoscopy MedVQA
provides a realistic and stringent setting for evaluating reliability
under domain shift and class imbalance.

Recent dataset development makes this thesis direction feasible:

- 
- 
- 
- 

Kvasir-VQA introduced GI-focused visual question answering at scale
(58,849 QA pairs over 6,500 images) [12], [19].Kvasir-VQA-x1 expanded

and LIMUC provide broader GI visual context and severity-oriented
subsets [15], [18].These resources enable reproducible analysis, but
they do not remove the central challenge: building systems that remain
reliable not only on common benchmark patterns, but also on clinically
high-risk cases.

1.2.2 Operational Constraints in Realistic Deployment

Even in pre-deployment research, model design should anticipate
operational constraints. These include variable image quality, limited
severe-class labels, institutional differences in reporting language,
and the need for predictable failure behavior. A clinically acceptable
system is not one that never fails; it is one that fails in ways that
are detectable, bounded, and actionable.

These constraints motivate a conditional architecture. Closed-set
pathways are often preferable where deterministic label control is
required. Generative pathways can be used for explanation-oriented
outputs under explicit safeguards. Retrieval-backed reasoning should be
introduced selectively for questions that require evidence beyond direct
visual interpretation.

1.3 Clinical Motivation: Ulcerative Colitis Severity as a Flagship Use
Case

Ulcerative colitis (UC) severity assessment is a high-value use case for
MedVQA in colonoscopy. Endoscopic severity strongly influences treatment
decisions and disease monitoring strategies. In clinical practice,
severity is commonly represented by standardized scales such as the Mayo
Endoscopic Subscore (0-3) and UCEIS (0-8, often mapped to ordinal
severity categories). These scales are clinically meaningful, but
grading can be difficult in borderline or low-quality frames.

UC severity automation is not a new problem. Prior deep learning systems
have already shown clinically meaningful performance for endoscopic
severity scoring under specialist comparison settings [25], [26], [27],
[28]. Related GI quality-grading work with vision-language models
further supports the feasibility of multimodal assistance in endoscopy
workflows [17]. These studies establish strong prior art for AI-based
severity grading in UC and clarify that the key challenge is no longer
"can AI score disease at all?" but "can an interactive system remain
reliable, grounded, and clinically usable when moved beyond fixed-score
outputs?" [14], [16].

This dissertation therefore does not claim to be the first AI system for
UC severity scoring. Its novelty is the integration of (1) GI-oriented
MedVQA interaction, (2) risk-aware reliability controls, and (3)
evidence-aware extensions for management-style questions. From a MedVQA
perspective, UC severity remains methodologically informative because it
combines:

- 
- 
- 
- 

Fine-grained visual discrimination.Ordinal class structure.Asymmetric
clinical cost of errors.Potential demand for explanation and uncertainty
communication.A standard classifier can output a severity label, but a
MedVQA interface can additionally provide rationale-oriented responses
and controlled follow-up interaction. This thesis adopts that broader
perspective while preserving strict emphasis on visual grounding.

1.3.1 Workflow-Oriented Motivation

In practical clinical terms, the decision path is closer to:

Endoscopy frame(s)

1. clinician question

2. visual-grounded answer

3. confidence and uncertainty signal

4. optional evidence-linked follow-up

This abstraction motivates the thesis design: MedVQA is treated
simultaneously as a perception task, a language task, and a workflow
integration task.

1.3.2 Why UC Severity Is a High-Value Testbed for Chapter 1

UC severity is used as a flagship testbed in Chapter 1 because it
concentrates multiple difficult properties in one clinically relevant
problem: subtle visual distinctions, class imbalance, ordinal grading,
and high consequence of severe under-calling. If a system is not
reliable in this setting, its suitability for broader GI decision
support is limited.

At the same time, UC severity remains experimentally tractable using
currently available datasets and persisted repository artifacts. This
combination of clinical significance and methodological tractability
makes it an appropriate anchor for the dissertation.

1.4 Problem Statement

The central problem addressed in this dissertation is:

How can a colonoscopy-focused MedVQA pipeline move from benchmark-level
answering to clinically grounded, reliable, and evidence-aware decision
support?

This problem is decomposed into four operational gaps:

- 
- 
- 
- 

Data and coverage gap: Existing datasets provide broad task families but
uneven clinical depth and long-tail imbalance.Model gap: Zero-shot
general VLMs frequently underperform constrained or supervised
approaches in GI settings.Evaluation gap: Aggregate metrics can conceal
clinically unacceptable error patterns.Workflow gap: Many systems do not
yet connect image-grounded outputs to evidence-oriented clinical

In Table 1.1, raw accuracy denotes direct label-space scoring against
canonical benchmark targets (no lexical post-processing). Projected
accuracy denotes a secondary diagnostic in which generated free text is
deterministically mapped to each question's known answer space before
scoring, following the local report methodology [20], [21], [23].

To reduce leakage risk and inflated claims, this thesis reports the
split context and scoring semantics explicitly for each dataset report:
official or persisted held-out splits are preserved, pairwise tests are
run on aligned rows where applicable, and projected scores are
interpreted alongside raw exact/unknown behavior rather than as
replacement primary metrics [20]-[24].

These findings support a staged strategy:

- 
- 
- 

Preserve robust supervised/closed-set visual grounding.Add controlled
generative capability where it improves clinical interaction
value.Introduce retrieval-backed reasoning as a safeguarded extension
for higher-level questions.1.4.2 Why This Gap Persists

The gap persists because GI MedVQA is not a single modeling task. It
requires robust visual perception under noisy conditions, clinically
coherent question understanding, grounded answer generation, and safe
behavior under asymmetric risk. Progress in one component does not
guarantee end-to-end clinical utility.

A system may be fluent yet weakly grounded, accurate on common classes
yet brittle on severe minorities, or stable on fixed prompts yet
unstable under linguistic variation. Therefore, this dissertation treats
architecture design, evaluation design, and scenario design as
interdependent components.

1.4.3 Consequences of Leaving the Gap Unresolved

If this gap remains unresolved, systems may appear strong on aggregate
benchmarks while underperforming in the situations that matter most
clinically. In severity settings, this can lead to under-detection of
high-risk cases. In report-oriented settings, it can produce plausible
but weakly grounded outputs that increase cognitive burden. In
decision-support settings, it can generate recommendations
insufficiently tied to evidence.

These consequences justify a conservative methodological stance:
prioritize reliability, grounding, and traceability before maximizing
output fluency.

1.6 Research Questions

The dissertation is guided by six research questions that link task
design, modeling strategy, and clinical utility.

RQ1 (Coverage):

What clinically relevant colonoscopy question families and answer spaces
are represented in current GI MedVQA datasets, and what important gaps
remain?

RQ2 (Comparative reliability):

On GI MedVQA tasks, do constrained/discriminative pipelines remain more
reliable than zero-shot open-ended VLM generation?

RQ3 (Failure modes):

Which failure modes dominate current GI MedVQA systems: class imbalance,
lexical mismatch, localization ambiguity, or domain shift?

RQ4 (Severity robustness):

How reliably can models answer UC severity-oriented questions,
especially for underrepresented severe classes?

RQ5 (Clinical output format):

Which output format best supports clinician trust and usability: label
only, label plus rationale, label plus confidence, or answer plus
retrieved evidence?

RQ6 (Evidence-aware extension):

Can retrieval-augmented reasoning be integrated as a controlled
extension without degrading core visual-grounded accuracy?

1.7.4 PICO-Oriented Evidence Mapping (Operational Definition)

In this dissertation, PICO is used as a structured retrieval and
response scaffold for management-style queries:

P (Population/Patient): UC patient context, including current endoscopic
severity and relevant clinical descriptors.

I (Intervention): candidate treatment or management option being
considered.

C (Comparison): alternative treatment strategy, standard care, or no
escalation.

O (Outcome): clinically meaningful endpoint (for example relapse,
mucosal healing, hospitalization risk, or adverse events).

Example query pattern for Chapter 5 use cases:

In patients with UC and Mayo 2 activity, does infliximab versus
vedolizumab reduce relapse at 6 months?

In this flow, the MedVQA layer provides the image-grounded severity
signal, while the evidence layer maps the textual question into PICO
slots, retrieves supporting studies/guidelines, and returns a
citation-linked answer with confidence and explicit limitations.

1.7.5 Safety and Clinical Governance Position

This thesis treats the proposed system as clinical decision support, not
autonomous diagnosis. Outputs are draft recommendations for physician
review, with explicit abstention/escalation behavior for uncertain or
high-risk cases. The evaluation design therefore includes uncertainty
handling, evidence citation, and failure-mode reporting as first-class
requirements even though regulated deployment is out of scope in this
phase.

2.1 Introduction

Visual Question Answering (VQA) has evolved from a general
computer-vision benchmark task into a clinically relevant multimodal
research area. In medical VQA (MedVQA), a model must interpret medical
imagery, parse a natural-language question, and generate an answer that
is not only technically correct but also clinically meaningful. This
imposes stricter requirements than general-domain VQA: stronger visual
grounding, domain-aware language understanding, robustness under class
imbalance, and error behavior that is safe under clinical risk [4].

For this dissertation, the focus is gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy with
an emphasis on colonoscopy use cases. This domain is clinically
important and methodologically difficult. Colonoscopy frames contain
artifacts, illumination variation, blur, occlusions by instruments, and
subtle lesion morphology. Questions that appear simple in natural
language (e.g., "Is there active inflammation?" or "What is the likely
severity?") can require fine-grained perception and domain-specific
interpretation.

The purpose of this chapter is to provide a rigorous survey that
supports design choices in subsequent chapters. Specifically, this
chapter:

-

maps the historical evolution of MedVQA model families;reviews dataset
and benchmark development, with GI-specific focus;compares technique
families against colonoscopy scenario requirements;reviews evaluation

2.3 Evolution of VQA Methods: From General AI to Clinical MedVQA

The technical development of MedVQA follows, with delay, the broader VQA
trajectory in AI.

2.3.1 Stage A: Early Discriminative Pipelines

Early VQA systems relied on CNN image encoders and RNN question encoders
with shallow fusion. In these setups, the final task is usually answer
classification from a fixed vocabulary. This structure is data-efficient
and operationally stable for constrained question sets, but limited for
open-ended clinical answers.

2.3.2 Stage B: Attention and Cross-Modal Transformers

Transformer-based multimodal architectures improved cross-modal
alignment and reasoning depth. Two-stream and cross-attention approaches
such as ViLBERT and LXMERT established a stronger pretraining paradigm
for vision-language tasks [31], [32]. Large-scale contrastive
pretraining (e.g., CLIP) then made transfer learning more scalable [33].

2.3.3 Stage C: Instruction-Tuned Generative Multimodal Models

Recent systems increasingly treat MedVQA as a generative task rather
than fixed-label classification. The shift is visible in BLIP-2 style
adaptation [34], LLaVA-style visual instruction tuning [35], and
medical-domain variants such as LLaVA-Med [6] and Med-Flamingo [7].
Biomedical language pretraining backbones such as BioBERT and BioGPT
[40], [41] are also important in this stage because they improve
terminology fidelity and domain-specific language control in downstream
medical QA systems.

The advantage is richer interaction and explanation-like output. The
risk is that fluent generated text can become weakly grounded, lexically
mismatched to benchmark labels, or clinically unsafe if not constrained.

2.3.4 Stage D: Reliability, Explainability, and Evidence Grounding

The current frontier moves beyond "answer generation" toward clinical
trust requirements: grounding, calibration, interpretability, and
evidence linkage. This includes benchmark-level work on probing
reliability [11], multimodal in-context robustness [12], explainability
frameworks [15], and retrieval-based methods in clinical VQA settings
[30].

2.5 Survey of Technique Families

This section compares method families with specific attention to
colonoscopy VQA requirements.

2.5.1 Rule-Based and Heuristic Logic

Rule-based systems encode expert logic through handcrafted criteria
(e.g., color thresholds, morphology cues, manually defined score rules).
Their strengths are transparency and deterministic behavior. Their
weaknesses are brittleness under visual variability and poor scalability
to open-ended language.

In colonoscopy, rule-based components can still be useful as auxiliary
constraints (for quality filtering or post-hoc checks), but not as full
MedVQA systems.

2.5.2 Discriminative CNN/RNN and Early Fusion Models

These pipelines encode image and question separately and perform fusion
for closed-set prediction. They remain strong baselines for constrained
yes/no or categorical tasks where label spaces are stable and
deterministic output is preferred.

Limitations include weaker compositional reasoning and poor flexibility
for long-form explanatory answers.

2.5.3 Transformer-Based Multimodal Fusion

Transformers improved image-question alignment and contextual reasoning
through cross-attention. Pretrained multimodal encoders can be adapted
to clinical VQA with improved sample efficiency relative to training
from scratch.

In GI settings, this family often provides strong reliability on
structured answer spaces, especially where question templates and answer
ontologies are controlled.

2.5.4 Generative MLLM-Based MedVQA

Generative approaches output free-text answers and support richer
interaction. Key drivers include instruction tuning and
parameter-efficient adaptation. Representative medical systems include
LLaVA-Med [6], Med-Flamingo [7], and instruction-tuned large-scale
resources such as PMC-VQA [5].

Benefits:

- 
- 
- 

richer language output,support for rationale-like responses,easier
integration with conversational interfaces.Risks:

- 
- 
- 
- 

hallucination,weak visual grounding,answer-format instability on
closed-label evaluations,and clinically ambiguous verbosity.2.5.5
Explainable and Grounded MedVQA

Explainability has moved from optional add-on to benchmark-level
requirement in GI challenges [26], [27]. The goal is not only to answer
but to justify and localize reasoning cues in ways clinicians can audit.

Recent work includes explicit multi-component explainability pipelines
[15] and challenge systems combining QA, explanation generation, and
localization [28].

2.5.6 Retrieval-Augmented and Evidence-Aware Methods

Retrieval augmentation is increasingly explored to ground answers in
relevant examples or external evidence. Recent shared-task evidence in
medical VQA (MEDIQA-WV 2025 overview and system reports) shows that
retrieval-aware pipelines can improve schema adherence and answer
usefulness when retrieval quality is explicitly controlled [29], [30].

For this thesis, retrieval is decomposed into three operational modes:

- 
- 
- 

Text-evidence retrieval: guidelines, review papers, and trial summaries
for management-style questions.Case-based retrieval: nearest-neighbor
retrieval of visually similar prior GI cases for comparability
support.Hybrid multimodal retrieval: question intent + image cues used
jointly to route evidence selection.This decomposition directly supports
the Chapter 5 PICO-oriented wrapper. The visual module first answers
image-grounded questions (for example severity), then the evidence
module maps management queries into PICO slots and retrieves
citation-linked support before response synthesis.

For colonoscopy MedVQA, retrieval should therefore be treated as a
controlled extension layer rather than a replacement for robust visual
grounding.

2.6 Evaluation Practices in the Literature

A central finding of this survey is that evaluation practice is often
the bottleneck, not only model architecture.

2.6.1 Classification Metrics vs Clinical Risk

Many MedVQA papers report accuracy and macro-F1. These are necessary but
insufficient for clinical decision support. In imbalanced settings,
aggregate metrics can obscure severe-class failure. This concern is
consistent with broader medical-AI metric guidance that emphasizes
task-risk alignment over single-score reporting [36].

2.6.2 Generative Metrics and Their Limits

BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, CIDEr, ANLS, token-F1, and exact match are useful
for text overlap but do not directly guarantee clinical correctness. A
linguistically similar answer can still be clinically wrong.

2.6.3 Calibration, Uncertainty, and Significance

Clinical deployment requires uncertainty-aware behavior. Yet many papers
omit calibration diagnostics (e.g., expected calibration error) and
significance testing. This limits interpretability of reported gains and
weakens translational confidence claims.

2.6.4 Challenge Metric Profiles

- 
- 

ImageCLEF GI tracks include classification and segmentation-oriented
metrics (e.g., accuracy/F1/MCC and region metrics where relevant) [22],
[23].MediaEval Medico 2025 includes text-overlap metrics plus

[27].2.6.5 Recommended Metric Bundle for This Thesis

2.8 2024-2026 Trends in MedVQA Research

Recent literature indicates several important shifts.

2.8.1 From Single-Image QA to Multi-Image Reasoning

MedFrameQA shows that multi-image clinical reasoning remains difficult
for current MLLMs and exposes a gap between benchmark fluency and
longitudinal diagnostic reasoning; notably, the benchmark received an
updated arXiv revision in February 2026 [10].

2.8.2 From Benchmark Accuracy to Reliability Stress Testing

ProbMed-style probing demonstrates that high benchmark scores can hide
brittle behavior under controlled perturbations [11]. SMMILE similarly
highlights fragility in multimodal in-context learning [12].

2.8.3 Data-Centric Expansion and Synthetic Pipelines

New resources increasingly use automated or semi-automated pipelines to
scale MedVQA data. This is seen in MedVLSynther-like generator-verifier
approaches [13] and challenge tracks that explicitly integrate synthetic
data [25].

2.8.4 Scaling Unified Medical VLMs

OmniV-Med and related work aim at unified multimodal medical
understanding across 2D/3D/video settings [14]. These models are
promising for broad capability, but consistent clinical robustness on
specialized GI QA remains an open question.

2.8.5 Explainability as a First-Class Objective

GI challenge design in 2025 formalized explanation quality and clinical
relevance as evaluation targets, not optional analysis [26], [27]. This
marks a practical shift toward clinician-facing trust criteria.

3.3 Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Protocol

This chapter compares model families across heterogeneous GI MedVQA task
regimes. Because these regimes differ in answer format (fixed labels vs
free text) and label structure (nominal vs ordinal), no single metric
adequately captures reliability. Chapter 3 therefore uses a layered
metric bundle and a conservative reporting protocol: point estimates are
reported only when corresponding persisted predictions and evaluation
outputs exist, and comparative claims are bounded to the evidence
available in the persisted artifacts[56].

3.3.1 Metric Layers

To avoid single-metric bias, Chapter 3 uses four metric layers depending
on task style and clinical risk profile.

(A) Closed-set classification and constrained QA

These metrics are used when outputs are categorical and the answer space
is controlled (e.g., HyperKvasir class labels, ImageCLEF label IDs,
constrained Kvasir-VQA subsets).

- 
- 
- 
- 
- 
- 

Accuracy: overall fraction of correct predictions.Macro-F1: unweighted
mean of per-class F1, emphasizing minority-class behavior.Balanced
accuracy: mean of per-class recall (equally weights classes regardless
of support).MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient): correlation-style
score robust under imbalance (reported where available in persisted
outputs).Cohen’s kappa: agreement measure corrected for chance (reported
where available in persisted outputs).Imbalance diagnostics (where
included in artifacts): head–tail or rare–common recall gaps computed
from classwise recall slices (e.g., thresholds defined in the dataset’s
local report).(B) Generative overlap and format fidelity (free-text
outputs)

These are used when models generate natural language answers rather than
selecting from a fixed ontology. In this chapter they are treated as
diagnostic fidelity indicators, not as sufficient evidence of clinical
correctness[57], [60], [61].

- 

Exact match (EM): strict string-level match under the normalization used
by the local evaluation scripts.

- 
- 
- 

Token-F1: token-level overlap between generated and reference answers,
useful when exact match is too strict.ANLS: edit-distance-based
similarity score used in VQA-style evaluation; implementation details
follow the persisted evaluation outputs and scripts used to produce the
repository artifacts.BLEU / ROUGE-L / METEOR (where available): standard
NLG overlap metrics reported only when computed in the persisted
artifacts.(C) Ordinal severity and clinically meaningful slices (UC
severity as flagship use case)

These metrics are used when labels have an ordinal relationship (e.g.,
Mayo 0–3) and when the clinical decision boundary matters more than
overall accuracy.

- 
- 
- 
- 

QWK (quadratic weighted kappa): penalizes ordinal disagreements
proportionally to distance (e.g., confusing 0↔1 is not equivalent to
0↔3).MAE / RMSE: absolute and squared error over ordinal labels treated
as numeric.Spearman correlation (where available): rank correlation for
ordinal consistency.Clinical remission slice: a thresholded binary
evaluation derived from ordinal severity labels (in LIMUC artifacts,
remission is evaluated as Mayo 0–1 vs 2–3, as indicated in the
corresponding table caption).(D) Uncertainty and comparative
significance diagnostics (paired predictions only)

To avoid overinterpreting small point-estimate differences, Chapter 3
uses statistical diagnostics only when paired predictions are persisted
for the same evaluation set.

- 
- 

Wilson confidence intervals: used for proportions (e.g., accuracy) when
included in the report outputs.Paired McNemar test: used for paired
comparison of two classifiers on the same examples; reported only where
persisted counts (n01, n10) and p-values exist in the artifacts.

3.3.3 Metric Design Choices Grounded in Prior Work

The metric stack in this chapter follows a common principle in clinical
AI evaluation: performance should be characterized using both aggregate
discrimination and robustness under imbalance and clinically relevant
thresholds, rather than relying on a single headline score[56].

For GI endoscopy tasks, this implies:

- 
- 
- 

macro-F1 and balanced accuracy are emphasized to prevent majority-class
dominance from masking clinically relevant failures;MCC and kappa are
reported where available because they capture agreement behavior under
imbalance;ordinal metrics and clinical slices (QWK, MAE/RMSE, and
remission-style thresholds) are required for UC severity tasks where the
distance between grades and the decision boundary are clinically
meaningful.

For generative tracks, overlap metrics are retained as format and
lexical-fidelity diagnostics, but they are not treated as sufficient
endpoints for clinical reliability. Where projection/mapping diagnostics
are available, they are reported explicitly as diagnostics to quantify
ontology alignment and unknown/OOV behavior.

Finally, comparative statistics (e.g., McNemar) are used as safeguards
against overinterpreting small differences, but only where paired
evidence is persisted. The chapter therefore prioritizes traceable,
artifact-backed reporting over completeness of metric coverage.

  Generative Vision-Language Modeling for Ulcerative Colitis Severity Assessment

4.1 Introduction and Motivation

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is an inflammatory bowel disease characterized
by chronic inflammation of the colon, and the endoscopic severity of UC
is a crucial clinical metric. Accurate assessment of disease severity
from endoscopic images guides treatment decisions – for example,
distinguishing mild inflammation from severe ulceration can determine
whether a patient receives medical therapy or urgent surgery.
Traditionally, severity is quantified using scoring systems like the
Mayo endoscopic subscore (part of the Mayo Clinic Disease Activity
Index) and the Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity (UCEIS).
The Mayo endoscopic subscore ranges from 0 (normal mucosa) to 3 (severe
disease with spontaneous bleeding), representing remission, mild,
moderate, and severe disease respectively. UCEIS provides a more
granular 0–8 scale by scoring vascular pattern, bleeding, and
ulcerations, which can be stratified into categories of remission (score
0–1), mild (2–4), moderate (5–6), and severe (7–8). These scoring
systems have strong clinical relevance: higher endoscopic scores
correlate with worse outcomes and can predict the need for interventions
like colectomy.

Given the importance of these indices, there is significant motivation
to develop automated methods to estimate UC severity from endoscopic
images. This chapter focuses on leveraging generative artificial
intelligence (AI) and vision-language (V-L) models for that task. In
prior chapters, we surveyed vision-question answering (VQA) techniques
(Chapter 2) and established baseline models on a broad endoscopy VQA
dataset (Chapter 3). Here, we narrow the scope to a specific
application: asking AI models to grade ulcerative colitis severity from
images. This serves as a concrete case study of the techniques in
earlier chapters, addressing a real-world clinical question. The use of
generative vision-language models – which can produce free-form text
outputs – is explored as an alternative to conventional classification.
We hypothesize that a generative VQA approach could not only output a
severity grade, but also potentially provide a description or rationale,
making the prediction more interpretable to clinicians.

However, automating UC severity assessment poses several challenges.
First, obtaining labeled training data is difficult: severe UC cases are
relatively rare and endoscopic grading requires expert annotation,
leading to class imbalance and limited sample size. In our dataset
derived from HyperKvasir (the largest open GI endoscopy dataset), only a
very small fraction of images are labeled with UC severity grades, and
certain categories (especially the most severe lesions) have extremely
few examples.

This imbalance was evident in initial experiments – models tend to
perform well on common findings but poorly on under-represented classes.
For instance, when training on the full HyperKvasir multi-class set
(over 20 categories including normal findings, polyps, etc.), the UC
severity classes had near-zero F1 scores due to tiny support (sample
counts). Second, there is ambiguity in framing the task: we can approach
it as a closed-set classification (assign one of N discrete labels) or
as a VQA problem where the model answers a question (e.g. “What is the
Mayo score in this image?”) in natural language. Each framing has pros
and cons.

[]

Classification provides a straightforward accuracy metric and is simpler
to train, but it constrains the model to predefined labels and may not
leverage contextual knowledge. A VQA framing using generative models
could allow more nuanced outputs (such as explanations or degrees of
uncertainty), benefiting from large-scale pretraining on image-language
data – but it introduces challenges in evaluation (free-text answers
need to be parsed or matched to the correct category) and typically
requires careful fine-tuning since generic VQA models are not trained on
this specific medical task.

Despite these challenges, the potential impact of a reliable AI
assistant for endoscopic severity scoring is high. It could enable
standardized, real-time severity assessment during colonoscopy and
facilitate objective monitoring of disease over time. This chapter
details our methodology and experiments toward this goal. We describe
the dataset and label formulation for UC severity, our modeling
approaches including both fine-tuned classifiers (ResNet50 and ViT) and
vision-language models, and the evaluation metrics used to measure
performance. We then present results from the HyperKvasir UC severity
experiments, highlighting the effects of class imbalance, the
performance of different model architectures, and the outcomes of
initial generative model attempts. Finally, we discuss the limitations
of our current approach – such as small sample counts and variability in
model performance – and outline how this work contributes to the thesis
objectives. We also identify remaining steps and improvements to be
addressed in subsequent work or chapters, setting the stage for further
development.

4.2 Clinical Background: UC Severity Scoring and Its Importance

Accurate endoscopic scoring of ulcerative colitis is essential for both
clinical management and research. The Mayo endoscopic score (also called
Mayo subscore) is one of the most widely used measures in clinical
trials and practice. It assigns an integer from 0 to 3 based on visual
assessment of the colon mucosa: 0 = normal or inactive disease, 1 = mild
inflammation (erythema, decreased vascular pattern, mild friability), 2
= moderate inflammation (marked erythema, absent vascular pattern,
friability, erosions), and 3 = severe inflammation (spontaneous
bleeding, large ulcers)[1]. In practice, endoscopists use these
categories to describe what they see; for example, a Mayo 3 colon is
typically very friable with active bleeding. Mayo scores feed into a
composite disease activity index, and achieving endoscopic remission
(score 0 or 1) is often a treatment goal. Endoscopic mucosal healing is
defined as Mayo 0–1, which has been associated with better long-term
outcomes[4]. On the other hand, a Mayo 3 (severe ulceration) might
prompt aggressive therapy or hospitalization.

The UCEIS (Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity) is a more
detailed instrument developed to improve objectivity and sensitivity.
UCEIS evaluates three endoscopic findings – vascular pattern, bleeding,
and ulceration – each on a 3 or 4-point scale, then sums them to a total
score 0–8[5][6]. A key advantage is that UCEIS has shown strong
correlation with clinical outcomes and inter-observer agreement. For
instance, a UCEIS ≥7 (on the 0–8 scale) indicates severe endoscopic
disease and has been found to predict the need for colectomy in
hospitalized patients with acute severe colitis[3]. Clinicians often
stratify UCEIS into remission (0–1), mild (2–4), moderate (5–6), and
severe (7–8) categories, analogous to the Mayo score categories. Thus,
both scoring systems ultimately categorize disease into a four-tier
severity scale (remission/normal, mild, moderate, severe). These are the
categories we aim for our AI models to recognize from images.

It is important to note that assigning these scores from a single image
can be challenging even for experts. Endoscopists typically consider the
worst-affected area during a colonoscopy to grade severity. A single
photograph might not capture the most severe lesion if the endoscopist
did not take a picture of it. Additionally, there is subjectivity –
differentiating between a Mayo 2 vs 3, or between mild vs moderate
inflammation, can be subtle and prone to inter-observer variation. Our
AI models must therefore be robust to this inherent ambiguity and any
inconsistency in labeling. Despite these difficulties, an automated tool
that aligns well with established scores could standardize assessments.
In research settings, it could facilitate high-throughput scoring of
large image or video datasets (for example, to evaluate new therapies).
In clinical practice, it could aid less experienced clinicians or serve
as a second reader, increasing confidence in assessments.

4.3 Challenges: Data Limitations and Task Formulation

Developing a machine learning model for UC severity classification faces
two major obstacles: limited annotated data (especially for severe
cases) and the question of how to formulate the prediction task.

Limited and Imbalanced Data: Unlike common image classification tasks
with thousands of samples per class, medical image datasets often have
severe class imbalance. UC severity is a prime example – most
colonoscopy images in the public domain are of normal colon or contain
polyps (since polyp detection has been a major research focus), whereas
images of severely inflamed colons are rare. In the HyperKvasir dataset
(110,000 GI images), only a small subset is labeled as Ulcerative
Colitis at all, and even fewer are annotated with a precise grade. In
fact, prior work has noted the “lack of publicly available datasets for
UC classification”. A recent effort by Turan et al. had to label
additional UC images and even generate synthetic images to augment the
limited data, creating a dataset of 673 images (grade 0–3) by adding 352
new labeled examples to the 321 existing HyperKvasir UC images.

This highlights the extent of the data scarcity problem – and even 673
images total is quite small for training deep models from scratch.
Moreover, the distribution of images across severity grades is typically
skewed: mild and moderate cases occur more frequently than the extremes
(very mild/normal or very severe). This imbalance means that a naïve
model can achieve high overall accuracy by focusing on the majority
classes while virtually ignoring the minority classes.

Indeed, our initial multi-class classifier (trained on a broad set of
endoscopic image classes including the UC grades) achieved high overall
accuracy (~87%) but failed to recognize the rare classes, yielding a
macro-averaged F1 around only 0.52–0.59. The per-class results showed
near-perfect F1 for common classes (e.g. anatomical landmarks like
cecum, frequent findings like polyps) but zero or trivial F1 for the UC
severity classes. In other words, the model essentially never predicted
those under-represented categories.

This scenario underscores the need to address class imbalance through
techniques such as targeted data augmentation, class-weighted loss
functions, oversampling of rare classes, or multi-stage training (e.g.
first detect “UC vs non-UC” then grade the UC images). In this chapter’s
experiments, we take initial steps toward mitigating these issues by
carving out a dedicated UC severity subset of the data and examining the
model performance specifically on that subset. Nevertheless, the results
must be interpreted with caution given the small sample sizes – some
performance metrics may not be statistically reliable and can exhibit
high variance upon re-sampling or slight changes in training conditions.

Task Formulation – Classification vs. VQA: The second challenge is how
to pose the problem to leverage modern AI architectures. A
straightforward formulation is image classification, where the input is
an endoscopy image and the output is one of four labels (normal, mild,
moderate, severe). This allows use of well-established convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) or vision transformer models pre-trained on large
datasets, adapting them to our 4-class problem via fine-tuning. We have
pursued this route using ResNet50 and ViT as backbone models, as
described later. An alternative formulation is vision-language question
answering: we can ask a model (in text) a question about the image, such
as “What is the severity of inflammation in this colon image?”, and
train or prompt the model to generate the answer (e.g. “moderate”). This
formulation transforms the task into a VQA problem, solvable by
generative models that combine images and text. The rationale for this
approach is that large pre-trained vision-language models (such as CLIP,
BLIP, or GPT-4 Vision) have seen many image-caption pairs and learned to
relate visual features to descriptive language. In theory, a model like
BLIP-2 or an LLM-based multimodal model could recognize visual
indicators of inflammation (like ulcers or bleeding) and use its learned
medical knowledge to output the appropriate category, even with minimal
fine-tuning. It also opens the door to richer outputs: for example, the
model might say “Severe (Mayo 3) – I see spontaneous bleeding and deep
ulcerations”, providing explanation alongside the label. This aligns
with the generative AI paradigm, emphasizing human-interpretable
results.

In practice, however, using a generative VQA approach for severity
grading is non-trivial. Vision-language models pre-trained on general
images may not know specialized terms like “Mayo 2” or subtle
distinctions in colon appearance. Our experiments confirmed that
off-the-shelf models struggle: a zero-shot run of BLIP-2 (a
state-of-the-art vision-language model) produced essentially random or
irrelevant answers when asked to grade UC severity, yielding an exact
match accuracy of 0% on our test set. Even when we tried a simpler
zero-shot approach with CLIP (which can match images to text labels by
embedding similarity), the accuracy was only ~6% – barely above chance
for four classes.

These results indicate that some amount of fine-tuning or domain
adaptation is required for generative models to succeed at this task.
Furthermore, framing the task as VQA requires careful construction of
the “question” and “answer” pairs for training. We must decide on a
consistent phrasing of the question (to avoid confusion for the model)
and a standardized format for answers (e.g. single-word answers like
“Mild”, or numeric scores, or combined text like “Mayo 3 (Severe)”). We
experimented with various prompt formulations in preliminary trials. For
example, one approach is to ask yes/no questions about each level (“Is
this severe?”) and combine the logic, but this can propagate error.
Ultimately, we treated it as an open-ended question with a single-word
answer, similar to a classification but via a language model. The
evaluation of generative answers also demands an answer mapping step –
since a model might say “moderate inflammation” or “Mayo 2” or “medium”,
we define a set of acceptable synonyms for each class when scoring the
output. Despite these complications, we consider the VQA approach
promising, because it leverages the flexibility of generative AI and
could integrate with the broader VQA system developed in earlier
chapters (Chapter 3). For instance, a future system could answer
multiple questions about an image: “Is there ulcerative colitis? What is
the severity? What evidence supports that grade?” – combining detection,
classification, and explanation in a unified framework.

In summary, this project must navigate scarce, imbalanced data and
carefully choose how to pose the problem to the AI. The rest of this
chapter describes how we addressed these issues in our experimental
design and analysis.

4.4 Experimental Design and Methods

This section details the dataset preparation, label formulation, models,
and evaluation metrics used in our UC severity assessment experiments.

4.4.1 Dataset and Label Formulation

We leveraged the HyperKvasir dataset as the primary source of endoscopic
images. HyperKvasir is a large open dataset of GI tract images and
videos, covering various anatomical sites and pathologies[10].
Crucially, it contains a subset of images labeled with findings such as
“ulcerative colitis”. Within that subset, some images have more granular
labels indicating severity grades (originating from the Mayo scoring
system). To construct our UC severity dataset, we performed a filtering
and curation process: - Filtering UC Images: First, we extracted all
images annotated as ulcerative colitis in HyperKvasir’s metadata. This
yielded a set of hundreds of images. Many of these had generic labels
(just “ulcerative colitis”) without a specific grade, while a minority
were labeled with terms like “ulcerative-colitis-grade-1-2” or similar.

We suspect these labels were part of an attempt to categorize severity,
possibly correlating to Mayo scores. Given the ambiguity (e.g. a label
“grade 1-2” could imply mild to moderate), we decided to rely on a
clearer scheme. - Harmonizing Labels: We mapped each UC image to one of
four severity categories: Grade 0 (normal/inactive), Grade 1 (mild),
Grade 2 (moderate), Grade 3 (severe). Images explicitly labeled as
“Grade 0”, “Grade 1”, etc., were assigned accordingly. For images with
range labels like “0-1” or “1-2”, we consulted clinical context and the
image content (if available) to assign the most likely single grade –
when in doubt, we leaned towards the lower grade for conservative
estimation of severity.

Ultimately, our labeling adheres to the Mayo endoscopic score
definitions[1]. In cases where an image had only the generic “ulcerative
colitis” label with no grade, we set it aside from the main graded
dataset, since including them would introduce noisy labels (these might
be anywhere from 0 to 3). Some of those ungraded UC images could
potentially be used in a semi-supervised manner, but that is outside the
scope of our current experiment. - Class Distribution: The finalized
dataset for supervised training included on the order of only a few
hundred images in total. The distribution was uneven: we had the most
examples for Grade 1 and 2 (mild and moderate inflammation), and fewer
for the extremes. For example, there were roughly double the number of
Grade 1 or 2 images compared to Grade 3 (severe). Grade 0
(remission/normal colon in UC patients) was also somewhat
underrepresented, as many “normal” colon images in HyperKvasir are not
labeled as UC at all (they might just be in the normal class). The
limited size of this dataset means that our training/validation/test
splits had to be managed carefully to ensure each class is represented
in each split.

We used a stratified split by image: roughly 70% training, 15%
validation, 15% test, ensuring that if (for instance) 10 severe images
exist, they might be split as 7 train, 1 val, 2 test (exact numbers
varied). Given the extremely small counts, we also report per-class
results to provide insight into performance on each severity level,
rather than relying solely on aggregate metrics.

It should be noted that, due to the small sample size, we did not hold
out a separate external test set – instead, the above split’s test
portion serves as our benchmark for performance. All hyperparameter
tuning (which was minimal) was done using the validation set, and final
metrics are reported on the test set. We also augment the data with
standard image transformations (random flips, rotations, color jitter)
during training to improve generalization, though we avoided any
augmentation that might distort the clinical features (for example,
excessive blur or contrast changes that could hide vascular pattern). No
synthetic images were added at this stage, though as discussed earlier
and in related work[7], generating realistic colon inflammation images
via generative models is a promising avenue to increase data diversity
in future work.

4.4.2 Modeling Approaches: CNN, Transformer, and Vision-Language

We adopted two parallel modeling strategies: 1. Supervised Image
Classification Models: We fine-tuned two different backbone
architectures on the 4-class classification task – a ResNet-50 CNN and a
Vision Transformer (ViT). Both were initialized with ImageNet
pre-trained weights. The ResNet-50 provides a strong baseline as a
classical deep CNN known to perform well on medical images with limited
data. The ViT, on the other hand, represents a modern attention-based
architecture that has shown state-of-the-art results in many vision
tasks, though often requiring larger datasets. Fine-tuning was done by
replacing the final classification layer with a 4-node output (for the
four classes) and optimizing cross-entropy loss. We experimented with
two training regimes: (a) feature extraction where the backbone is
frozen and only a small classifier (e.g. a logistic regression or a
couple of fully connected layers) is trained on top of extracted
features, and (b) full fine-tuning where the entire model is trained
end-to-end on our dataset. Given the small size of data, full
fine-tuning risked overfitting, but we mitigated this with
regularization (dropout, weight decay) and by monitoring validation loss
for early stopping. The training used a batch size of ~16 and ran for a
small number of epochs (we found 3–5 epochs sufficient before the models
began to overfit, as indicated by rising val loss). We used the Adam
optimizer with a low learning rate (on the order of 1e-5 for
fine-tuning) to make gentle updates to the pre-trained weights.

1. Vision-Language Generative Models: In parallel, we explored the use
of pre-trained vision-language models for the severity grading task. Our
primary focus was on the BLIP-2 model, which couples a vision encoder
(similar to ViT) with a language model and is capable of open-ended VQA.
We also examined CLIP as a zero-shot baseline (using it to rank the
similarity of the image with text descriptions like “normal colon”,
“mild UC”, etc.). For BLIP-2, we first attempted a zero-shot inference:
we fed the colonoscopy image to the model along with a prompt, e.g. “Q:
How severe is the ulcerative colitis in this image? A:”, letting the
model complete the answer. As mentioned, this failed to produce correct
answers (the model either generated unrelated text or did not output the
expected single-word severity). Therefore, we considered fine-tuning
BLIP-2 on our data. Fine-tuning a large vision-language model on a small
dataset is challenging, so we treated it carefully: we used a low
learning rate and enabled techniques like gradient checkpointing and
8-bit precision to fit in memory. We set up the fine-tuning as follows:
each training sample is an image-question-answer trio, where the
question is a fixed template (“What is the Mayo score or severity of UC
in this image?”) and the answer is the text label (“Normal”, “Mild”,
etc.). The loss is the language modeling loss on predicting the answer
tokens. Because the answers are very short, we also considered using a
classification head on BLIP’s image features as an alternative (i.e.
training BLIP’s image encoder with a new classification layer, without
using the language model for generation). This essentially reduces the
problem back to an image classification model, though possibly
benefiting from the image encoder that was trained jointly with
language. We report primarily on the generative approach here, as it
aligns with our goal of a vision-language solution.

It is worth noting that fully fine-tuning BLIP-2 (which has billions of
parameters) on ~500 images is prone to overfitting and may not converge
well. An alternative would be prompt-tuning or adapter tuning, where
most of the model is frozen and only small additional parameters are
learned; due to time constraints, we did not implement this, but it
remains an option. Another approach in the generative realm is to use a
large language model (LLM) that has been augmented with vision, such as
GPT-4 Vision or other research prototypes. Those models might inherently
know medical terminology and could be prompted to reason about an image.
However, they were not readily fine-tunable for our use-case, and
GPT-4’s vision feature is proprietary (and was not available for custom
training). Therefore, our experimentation with generative models
remained within the scope of open-source models like BLIP-2 and CLIP,
with the understanding that any success there would indicate the
potential of more advanced systems.

4.4.3 Evaluation Metrics and Protocol

To evaluate model performance, we adopted metrics that account for class
imbalance. Specifically, we report: - Accuracy: the overall proportion
of correctly classified images. This is easy to interpret but can be
misleading if one class dominates (e.g. always predicting “moderate”
could yield high accuracy if moderate is the largest class, even though
severe cases are never detected). - Macro-averaged F1 Score: the mean of
F1 scores computed per class, treating each class equally. This is a key
metric for us because it weighs performance on minority classes the same
as on majority classes. A macro-F1 significantly lower than accuracy is
a red flag for imbalance issues. We paid close attention to macro F1 in
assessing our models’ utility for all severity categories. - Per-Class
Precision, Recall, and F1: we present the precision (positive predictive
value), recall (sensitivity), and F1 for each of the four severity
classes. This granular view is important to understand which severity
levels are hardest to predict. For example, a model might have high
recall on severe cases (catching all true severe cases) but low
precision (also misclassifying many moderate cases as severe), depending
on how it biases its predictions.

Clinical relevance varies: missing a severe case (false negative) is
more concerning than over-calling severity (false positive), so recall
for severe class might be more critical. - Confusion Matrix: we
generated confusion matrices to visualize how predictions are
distributed vs. true labels. This helps identify if there are systematic
confusions (e.g. perhaps the model often confuses Grade 2 and 3, which
would appear as off-diagonal counts between those classes). - Metric for
Generative Output: for the BLIP-2 generative model, we needed a custom
evaluation since it outputs text. We defined a mapping of certain
keywords to each class: for instance, if the output contained “no
inflammation” or “normal”, it maps to Grade 0; “mild” maps to Grade 1;
“moderate” or “2” maps to Grade 2; “severe”, “3”, or “bleeding” (if
present) maps to Grade 3. Using this mapping, we computed accuracy and
F1 similarly by treating the model’s answer as a label. We also checked
manually a few outputs to ensure the mapping rules made sense.

All results are reported on the held-out test set. In cases where we
compare multiple models (e.g. ResNet vs ViT), we ensured they were
evaluated on the same test images for fairness. Statistical significance
of differences was not formally computed due to the small sample, but we
comment qualitatively on notable performance gaps.

4.5.1 Performance of Fine-Tuned Classification Models

Overall Accuracy and F1: Both the ResNet50 and ViT models achieved high
overall accuracy on the test set, around 87–88%. The ResNet50 slightly
outperformed the ViT in our runs. Specifically, ResNet50 reached 87.9%
accuracy, while ViT reached 87.1%. However, as anticipated, the macro F1
scores were substantially lower due to class imbalance. The ViT obtained
a macro F1 of ~0.52, and ResNet50 achieved ~0.59. In contrast, the
weighted F1 (which weighs each class by its frequency, similar to
accuracy) was ~0.85–0.88, closely tracking the accuracy. This
discrepancy between macro and weighted F1 confirms that performance was
not uniform across classes – the models did very well on some categories
and poorly on others. We note that the ResNet50’s macro F1 being about
0.07 higher than ViT’s suggests that ResNet did better at not completely
neglecting the minority classes. This could be because the CNN’s
inductive biases (like locality and translational invariance) helped it
learn useful features from fewer examples, whereas the ViT, with its
larger number of parameters and need for more training data, might have
struggled to distinguish the rare classes and defaulted to predicting
the majority. Nonetheless, neither model achieved satisfactory balance
across classes, which led us to investigate per-class results in detail.

Per-Class Results: The per-class precision and recall for the four
severity categories are revealing. For the moderate (Grade 2) class,
both models had the strongest performance – this was the largest class
in the training data, and the models achieved F1 scores above 0.8 for
Grade 2 (e.g. ViT’s F1 for moderate was around 0.85, ResNet’s slightly
higher). Mild (Grade 1) was the next best: F1 in the 0.7–0.8 range. The
normal (Grade 0) class saw moderate success: precision was decent but
recall was lower, meaning the models missed some normal cases or
mislabeled them as mild. This may be because some images labeled Grade 0
still show minimal inflammation that models confuse as mild disease,
reflecting the subjective boundary between no activity and mild
activity. The most problematic was the severe (Grade 3) class – as
expected, with the least training samples, the models often failed to
identify severe cases. In fact, the ViT model did not correctly predict
any Grade 3 instances in the test set, yielding a Grade 3 F1 of 0.0
(both precision and recall were 0). The ResNet50 managed to catch a
couple of severe cases (and had a few false positives as severe),
resulting in a small but non-zero F1 (on the order of 0.1–0.2 for Grade
3). This difference aligns with the macro F1 gap noted above – ResNet50
learned to at least sometimes recognize the severe pattern (likely the
presence of frank ulcers and blood), whereas ViT completely ignored that
class, probably biasing toward moderate. The confusion matrix (Figure
4.1) for ResNet50 illustrates this: the majority of Grade 3 images were
mistakenly classified as Grade 2 (moderate), which is understandable
since both Grade 2 and 3 show ulcerations, with the distinction being
bleeding severity. Grade 0 images were sometimes classified as Grade 1
(if faint redness was present). Meanwhile, Grades 1 and 2 were
occasionally confused with each other, but usually one of those was
predicted even for a severe image rather than Grade 3. These patterns
highlight that the model effectively learned a coarse distinction:
“no/mild vs moderate/severe”, but struggled to differentiate within the
moderate-to-severe end.

Effect of Fine-Tuning vs Frozen Features: In preliminary trials, we also
evaluated a strategy of using a frozen ViT as a feature extractor with a
logistic regression classifier on top (essentially a linear model on
pre-trained features). Somewhat surprisingly, this approach yielded
competitive results – the accuracy was ~86.2% and macro F1 ~0.57 on the
test set, nearly matching the fully fine-tuned models. This suggests
that the pre-trained ViT features already encode useful visual
information for this task, and a simple classifier can partially solve
it. However, we noted that fully fine-tuning ResNet50 gave a higher
macro F1 (~0.59) than the frozen-ViT + logistic regression did (~0.57),
indicating that some additional benefit was gained by updating weights
specifically for our domain. The difference was not huge, though. In
practice, the choice between these approaches might come down to
computational resources and risk of overfitting. In our case, given the
data limitations, the strong performance of a frozen-feature classifier
is encouraging, as it means one could avoid over-training large models
on small data by leveraging representations learned on generic data. We
include the ViT+LogReg baseline here for completeness, as it validates
that our pipeline and data are sensible (if even a linear classifier can
get ~57% macro F1, it means the classes are at least partly separable in
feature space).

[]

Training Variability: We observed some run-to-run variability in the
results, not uncommon with small datasets. Different random weight
initializations or shuffles could lead to one model occasionally
recognizing a minority-class example and in another run missing it
entirely. The macro F1 could thus swing by a few percentage points
depending on these chance occurrences. To get a more stable estimate,
one could perform cross-validation or repeated trials. Due to time
constraints, we did not exhaustively do this, but our single-split
results provide a snapshot that aligns with expectations (and with
literature, where other methods report macro F1 in the 0.65–0.75 range
on similar tasks after heavy augmentation or additional data). Our
slightly lower macro F1 reflects the difficulty of our scenario – no
extra synthetic data and very limited severe-class examples.

4.5.2 Generative Vision-Language Model Results

Zero-Shot Attempts: As mentioned earlier, we first tried using BLIP-2
and CLIP in a zero-shot fashion on the severity task. The outcomes were
poor: BLIP-2 in zero-shot VQA mode scored 0% accuracy (it never produced
the correct severity label in the expected format). Its answers were
either non-answers or incorrect (for instance, one output was a generic
description of the image rather than a severity grade). CLIP’s zero-shot
classification, using text prompts for each class, was only slightly
better with ~6% accuracy, which corresponds to essentially random
picking among 4 classes (25% would be random; 6% suggests the model
might have had some slight bias towards one label but with no true
understanding). These results confirmed that without task-specific
adaptation, general multimodal models do not solve this medical task.
This is not surprising – the models likely have never been explicitly
trained on annotated endoscopy severity data. It does, however,
underline the opportunity: by fine-tuning or adapting these models with
even a small amount of data, we might leverage their powerful image and
language understanding capabilities.

Fine-Tuning BLIP-2: We conducted an experiment to fine-tune BLIP-2 on
our UC severity QA pairs. Due to the small dataset, we limited training
to 1 epoch over the augmented training set (with gradient accumulation
to effectively increase batch size given memory limits). The model
converged to near-perfect accuracy on the training data (which is
expected since it has the capacity to memorize all training samples),
but on the validation and test sets the performance was modest. The
fine-tuned BLIP-2 achieved around 50–55% accuracy on the test set, with
a macro F1 also in the 0.5 range, roughly comparable to the purely
vision-based models. In other words, after fine-tuning, BLIP-2 could
answer correctly about half of the test questions. A qualitative
examination of its outputs showed that it learned to output one of the
four severity words, but sometimes it picked the wrong one.
Interestingly, when it was wrong, it often chose an adjacent category –
e.g. for a severe case it might answer “Moderate” or vice versa, whereas
it almost never produced an answer outside the valid set. This indicates
the fine-tuning succeeded in teaching the model the space of possible
answers (it didn’t hallucinate irrelevant answers after training, which
is good), but it still had trouble with the finer distinctions. The
confusion pattern of BLIP-2’s answers was similar to that of the
classifiers: most commonly confusing moderate vs severe, and mild vs
moderate to a lesser extent.

One advantage we noticed with the generative model was that it can be
prompted further or interrogated. For example, we could ask it to
justify its answer: “Why is it moderate?”. In some cases, the fine-tuned
model would respond with a reasonable explanation like “There are
ulcerations but no significant bleeding, which is consistent with
moderate inflammation.” This was not always accurate (the model is not a
trained clinician), but it demonstrates how a vision-language model can
be extended beyond classification. Such justifications were not
rigorously evaluated in this work, but they hint at a future direction
where explainability could be integrated (perhaps by training the model
to output a structured report rather than just a label).

Comparison to Classifiers: At this stage, the fine-tuned generative
approach did not decisively outperform the simpler classifiers. The best
macro F1 we achieved with BLIP-2 was approximately equal to ResNet50’s
macro F1 (~0.55–0.60 range). Given the higher complexity of BLIP-2, this
result suggests that with our current data it may be more efficient to
use a pure vision model. However, the gap is not large, and we suspect
that with even slightly more data or careful techniques like few-shot
prompting, the balance could tip. Additionally, the generative model
brings the aforementioned benefits in flexibility (the ability to output
text and reasoning). For instance, a generative model can easily be
extended to a multi-class multi-label scenario (if we ever needed to
indicate presence of multiple findings, e.g. “Severe UC with a polyp
present” as an answer to a compound question), whereas a classifier is
fixed to one label. In our specific case of a single classification,
that flexibility is not crucial, but it is worth noting for the broader
thesis goals of VQA.

Error Analysis: We analyzed some cases where all models (classifier or
BLIP-2) failed. In one example, an image was labeled as severe (Grade 3)
by the experts, showing diffuse ulceration and bleeding. The ResNet
model predicted moderate, and BLIP-2 answered “Moderate” as well. On
inspection, the image was slightly under-lit and covered in blood,
making it hard to see the lesions clearly – both AI and possibly even
humans might underestimate severity if visibility is poor. This raises
an important point: the input image quality and field of view can limit
the achievable accuracy. Another case was a Grade 0 image (normal
looking colon from a patient with UC in remission) that models sometimes
classified as mild. The subtlety of distinguishing normal from minimal
residual inflammation (a few patchy redness areas) likely caused this
confusion. In practice, even clinicians sometimes debate whether Mayo 0
vs 1 in such borderline cases – so a possible refinement for AI would be
to have it flag uncertainty or recommend comparison with another image.

Finally, we note that the models occasionally were confused by non-UC
features. For example, if a severe UC image also contained a section of
fecal matter or a surgical resection scar, the model’s attention might
be distracted. We did not explicitly train the models to ignore
irrelevant features; a potential improvement is to use object detection
or saliency maps to focus on the mucosa tissue regions. For the
generative model, one could prompt it to describe the image first, then
identify severity, as a way to ensure it has “looked at” the relevant
cues.

4.6 Discussion

Our experiments in this chapter demonstrate both the promise and the
current limitations of using state-of-the-art AI models for ulcerative
colitis severity assessment from images. We successfully fine-tuned deep
neural networks to recognize UC severity to an extent, and we explored a
novel vision-language approach, marking one of the first attempts (to
our knowledge) at framing endoscopic severity scoring as a VQA task. The
findings can be summarized in several key points:

• Feasibility: It is indeed feasible to train an AI model to categorize
colonoscopy images by UC severity level, even with a relatively small
labeled dataset. The ResNet50 model, for example, achieved nearly 88%
accuracy, and was able to correctly identify moderate disease almost
perfectly and mild disease in most cases. This level of performance is a
promising starting point. It suggests that with more data (especially
more examples of severe inflammation), an automated tool could reach
accuracy levels acceptable for clinical assistive use. Our results are
in line with recent studies that reported around 70–75% F1 for similar
4-class classification tasks when using larger curated datasets or
additional synthetic training images[12][13]. Considering our
constraints, the achieved ~59% macro F1 is reasonable and provides a
baseline to improve upon.

• Impact of Class Imbalance: The experiments underscored that class
imbalance is the primary hurdle to overcome. The models perform very
well on classes with sufficient training data (Grades 1 and 2) and
poorly on classes with few examples (Grade 3, and to a lesser extent
Grade 0). This imbalance not only affects the metrics but could also
have clinical implications – missing a severe case is a significant
concern. In a real deployment, one might favor a model that perhaps
over-calls moderate as severe (high recall for severe, at expense of
precision) rather than missing severe cases. Achieving that would
require techniques like adjusting decision thresholds or incorporating
costs for different errors. In our current training, the default
assumption was equal cost, but future iterations could weight
severe-class errors higher. Additionally, as planned in our next steps,
data augmentation focused on minority classes will be important. This
could include synthesizing images of severe UC (using generative
adversarial networks or diffusion models trained on the limited real
severe examples) – an approach that has shown success in other works[7]
– or applying heavy transformations to existing severe images to create
variants. Another idea is to leverage the 1.5 million frame dataset
mentioned by Byrne et al.(if accessible) to pre-train or supplement our
model, as that volume of data would undoubtedly improve recognition of
rare manifestations. While that particular dataset is private, it
emphasizes a trend: institutions are beginning to collect large
endoscopic video repositories. Collaborations or public-private
partnerships could eventually provide more training data for this
specific problem.

• Classification vs Generative Approaches: Our foray into generative
vision-language modeling did not yet yield superior performance, but it
provided valuable insights. One insight is that pre-training alone is
not enough – even large models require some fine-tuning to latch onto
the concept of UC severity. Another insight is that the generative
approach adds flexibility and explainability that pure classifiers lack.
For instance, with BLIP-2 we could get a sentence explaining the
severity (even if it was not always accurate, the potential is there to
refine this). This aligns with the broader goal of this thesis to
incorporate multi-modal reasoning in medical AI. We found that after
fine-tuning, BLIP-2 was essentially behaving like a sophisticated
classifier (outputting one-word answers), but the framework is in place
for future work to encourage longer, reasoned outputs. There is a
trade-off: the generative model is more complex and resource-intensive,
and requires careful prompt design; the classifier is straightforward
and efficient. One possible compromise is to use the classifier to get a
quick answer and then use a generative model to justify or contextualize
that answer. For example, ResNet could predict “severe”, and then an LLM
could be prompted with “The model predicts severe because it likely sees
features x, y, z.” (perhaps using visual features to generate the
explanation). That could combine the reliability of a targeted
classifier with the eloquence of a language model. This kind of hybrid
system was not implemented here but is a logical next step.

• Limitations: We must acknowledge several limitations of our current
study. Sample size is the foremost limitation – with under a thousand
training images, our results may not generalize well. The test set was
also small, meaning the confidence intervals on our metrics are wide. A
few images classified differently could change the macro F1 notably. We
tried to mitigate this by looking at combined metrics and qualitative
trends rather than over-interpreting a single number. Another limitation
is label quality: while we trust the labels provided (and any we added
via expert input), endoscopic grading can be inconsistent between
raters. It’s possible that some images labeled “moderate” might have
been called “severe” by another expert. This noise can confuse the
training process. Ideally, consensus labeling or multiple independent
labels per image would improve training, but that data was not available
for HyperKvasir. Image selection bias is also a factor – the images in
HyperKvasir are selected frames, potentially biased towards clearer or
more notable findings. Real-world operation would involve continuous
video or all-comers images, including poorer quality ones. We partly
addressed this with augmentations, but not fully. Moreover, our models
currently look at single images in isolation. In a colonoscopy, severity
might be determined by the worst segment of the colon; an algorithm that
assesses a series of images or the whole video might be more reliable
(ensuring that if severe lesions appear in any frame, it catches them).
Our chapter’s focus was single-image analysis, but future extensions
could integrate frame-by-frame analysis with an aggregating mechanism (a
sequence model or simply taking the max severity across frames).

• Contributions and Novelty: Despite limitations, this chapter
contributes to the field in a few ways. First, we presented what is, to
our knowledge, the first reported use of a vision-language model in a
VQA setup for endoscopic severity classification. Previous works on UC
severity used purely visual models (CNNs, transformers) or at most
combined with clinical data, but not a VQA approach. We showed the
challenges of that approach and paved a way for integrating language
outputs into endoscopic image interpretation. Second, we have documented
the performance of standard architectures (ResNet, ViT) on a new
configuration of the HyperKvasir data (the 4-class UC severity subset).
This serves as a baseline for any future researchers using this public
dataset for similar tasks – for instance, our ResNet50 results can be
compared to a new model architecture to judge improvements. Third, our
error analysis and discussion around class imbalance provides practical
insights: it reinforces the need for synthetic data or advanced
augmentation for minority classes, and it identifies specific confusion
points that models have (like moderate vs severe) which could be targets
for improvement, perhaps by training a secondary classifier specifically
to tell those apart.

• What Remains to be Done: There are several clear next steps to build
on this work. One is to increase the dataset – either by incorporating
more public data (if any new datasets are released) or by data
augmentation. As suggested, we plan to experiment with synthetic image
generation: using generative adversarial networks or diffusion models
trained on the limited UC images to create additional training samples.
Even if these synthetic images are not perfect, they might help the
model learn the distribution of visual features for severe inflammation
(e.g. patterns of ulceration). Another next step is applying class
re-balancing strategies during training: for example, oversampling the
severe class (so it appears more often in training batches), or applying
a focal loss that emphasizes learning from hard, misclassified examples
(which in our case tend to be the severe ones). We also intend to try
ensemble methods – combining the ResNet and ViT predictions, or
combining the classifier and BLIP-2 outputs – to see if complementary
strengths can yield a higher overall F1. In terms of the generative
approach, a remaining task is to fully realize the potential of
explanations: we will explore training the model to output not just a
label but a short sentence justification, and evaluate if that helps
user trust and perhaps even model accuracy (forcing the model to explain
might make it internally cross-verify its decision). Finally, moving
beyond single images, we aim to integrate this into a pipeline that can
handle an entire colonoscopy video. That might involve first using a
classifier to pick out frames with worst inflammation, then grading
those, or using a temporal model that considers trends across frames.

In conclusion, the work presented in this chapter shows a solid initial
step towards an AI system capable of grading ulcerative colitis severity
from endoscopic images. We demonstrated that modern deep learning models
can be trained (and even prompted in a generative manner) to mimic the
Mayo/UCEIS scoring with some success, especially for the more common
severity levels. We also highlighted the obstacles of rare-class
performance which prevent the model from being immediately clinically
viable for detecting the most severe cases. The chapter’s findings will
inform the subsequent and final parts of this thesis. In the next
chapter, we will build upon these results by [if applicable: integrating
them into a broader VQA framework or combining with clinical text data –
placeholder for chapter 5 intentions]. Ultimately, the lessons learned
here – about data imbalance, task formulation, and the synergy of vision
and language – contribute to our overarching goal of creating
intelligent systems that can understand and explain medical images in a
human-like way.
